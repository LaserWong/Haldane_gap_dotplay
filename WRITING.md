# Scientific writing

Project documentation uses natural scientific English.
Organize each explanation around the research question, the result, its evidence, and the conditions under which it holds.
The latest explicit requirements for a task determine its scope and format.

## Claims and evidence

State the scientific object and the main finding early.
Preserve the Hamiltonian, normalization, boundary conditions, parameter domain, size range, quantifiers, and order of limits.
Distinguish exact identities, rigorous bounds, controlled approximations, finite-size observations, fits, and conjectures.
Do not turn a numerical observation into a theorem or a replay into a new result.
Place uncertainty and limitations beside the claims they qualify.

Give definitions before their first substantive use.
Present the decisive reasoning in the main explanation and use appendices for the algebra and conditions needed to check it.
Show the steps that determine signs, coefficients, cancellations, normalization, and operator order.
State the applicable form and assumptions of a standard tool without repeating unrelated textbook material.

## Sentences and structure

Use plain, stable terminology and concrete verbs.
Begin with a short, clear topic and bring the complete main predicate forward.
Place lengthy methods and conditions afterward when this makes their scope clearer.
Use an object as the subject for its properties, use “we” for an actual choice or operation, and use a natural passive when it preserves the topic.
Choose the voice for the meaning rather than a fixed pattern.

Connect paragraphs through the quantities and conclusions that the argument actually uses.
Avoid promotional claims, repeated section previews, and statements that merely announce completion.
Use continuous prose for dependent reasoning, lists for parallel conditions or practical steps, and tables for genuine comparisons.
Headings identify the object, method, calculation, or result.

Do not use semicolons in new or revised natural-language text, including comments.
Avoid parenthetical em dashes, decorative quotation marks, and colons in scientific prose.
Keep punctuation required by mathematics, programming languages, file formats, and exact source quotations.

## Notation, figures, and sources

Preserve correct symbols, fonts, indices, arguments, units, macros, labels, citation keys, and operator order.
Keep the full Hilbert-space dimension, linear trial-space dimension, and MPS bond dimension distinct.
Use consistent model names, especially for the ordinary and fully frustrated bilayers.
Do not rename technical identifiers merely to vary the prose.

Display the principal relations and define short secondary quantities near their use.
Treat equations as parts of sentences.
In LaTeX source, start each complete prose sentence on a new line without creating an unnecessary paragraph.
Separate source line breaks, paragraph boundaries, and displayed-equation breaks.
Use `Eq.` and `Eqs.` for numbered equation references.

Figures and tables must preserve the definitions, units, parameters, and evidence level used in the text.
Explain error bars, fits, and numerical limits when they affect interpretation.
Cite the source that supports the specific nearby claim.
Do not invent missing bibliographic details, results, or checks.

## Repository records

Use repository-relative links in project documentation and plain-text hashes for commit identifiers.
Keep machine-readable field names, paths, provenance hashes, and numerical values stable during language-only edits.
Describe historical pauses, resumptions, and verification results with their dates.
Do not rewrite a historical status as a current one.

Preserve raw logs, numerical data, certificates, manifests, and archived originals byte for byte unless a separate task explicitly requires changing them.
Provide translations and explanations in maintained documentation rather than altering evidence.
Check that claimed files and dependencies exist, and report only the verification actually performed.
